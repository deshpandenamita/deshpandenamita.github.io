The Rocky Mountain Institute (RMI) Innovation Center: Passive Design Case Study

An analysis of how a net-zero office in the Colorado mountains heats itself with sun, insulation and airtightness instead of a furnace.

01

The building

The RMI Innovation Center is a 15,600 ft² net-zero office in Basalt, Colorado, completed in 2017. I chose it from sustainable architecture case studies for my MS Sustainable Building Systems coursework. It relies on south-facing glazing and a superinsulated envelope to stay warm through sub-zero Rocky Mountain winters without a conventional heating system.

The south facade has a 52% window-to-wall ratio to capture winter sun, and automated exterior blinds control solar gain.

02

Climate analysis

I used weather data from the Aspen-Pitkin County Airport (Sardy Field) station. It shows a cold continental climate. Winter daytime temperatures average -5°C and night lows reach -15°C. Diurnal swings of 12 to 15°C allow thermal mass to store solar gains for the night.

Relative humidity stays dry at 40 to 60% year-round, so an airtight envelope carries little condensation risk. Global horizontal irradiation remains substantial in the peak heating months. On the monthly diurnal chart, winter daytime peaks climb above freezing even when averages are below it. Wind averaging 3 to 5 m/s makes airtightness essential to limit infiltration losses, while mild summers suit night flushing.

03

Sun and comfort

The sun path diagram shows the December solstice sun reaching only about 25° at noon. Low winter sun reaches deep into south-facing rooms, while higher summer sun can be blocked by overhangs and blinds.

The psychrometric chart shows a dense cluster of cold, dry hours below the comfort zone in the heating months. About half of all hours need heating, and high irradiation arrives exactly when temperatures drop below freezing. A passive solar overlay shifts roughly 50% of annual hours into comfort.

04

How the passive system works

The building's systems work together to capture, store and keep solar heat.

  • Capture: the 52% glazed south facade admits low winter sun, which warms the concrete slab floors and interior surfaces.
  • Retain: structural insulated panels give R-50 walls, and the envelope reaches 0.1 CFM50/ft² of air leakage against -15°C nights.
  • Control: automated exterior blinds stay open in winter and close against high summer sun to prevent overheating.
  • Store: the 12 to 15°C daily temperature swing lets thermal mass release daytime heat slowly through the night.
05

Performance and evaluation

The building reaches verified net-zero performance at 35 kBtu/ft²/yr, 80% below regional code baselines, with no furnace or boiler. Photovoltaic panels produce a surplus over the year, and the biophilic atrium and south daylight support occupant comfort.

As a student focused on Boston-area buildings, I see RMI as a strong model for cold climates with large daily temperature swings. Its automated controls reduce the risk that occupant behaviour undermines performance, and the approach can scale to urban offices pursuing LEED v5. The one long-term concern I see is keeping the envelope airtight over time, although Passive House-level detailing suggests it will hold up.

What I took away

  • Passive solar heating works best when the climate pairs cold temperatures with strong sun and large day-night swings.
  • Glazing, insulation, airtightness and automated shading only remove the need for a heating plant when they are designed together.
  • The approach can transfer to cloudier Boston conditions if it is detailed with automation and an airtight envelope.
