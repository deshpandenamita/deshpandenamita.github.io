Climate & water

Bangalore Climate: Experience, Data and Design Strategies

A climate study of my home city that sets lived experience against Climate Consultant data and turns both into passive design strategies.

Tools
Climate Consultant
Location
Bangalore, Karnataka, India
Contents
  1. Qualitative experience
  2. Data-driven analysis

I lived in Bangalore for 22 years before moving to the US for my master's, so I chose it as the location for this climate study. I first wrote down how its climate felt, then checked that memory against weather data in Climate Consultant. From the two together I set out seven groups of design strategies suited to the city.

01

Qualitative experience

I remember Bangalore's climate as moderate and balanced. Summer afternoons between March and May could get warm, but mornings and evenings stayed pleasant, and winters were mild and never uncomfortably cold.

Humidity was most noticeable in the monsoon, when the air grew heavy. Skies were clear and bright in winter and early summer, then turned overcast and grey once the rains began. Between June and September there were daily showers that cooled the ground, followed by long dry periods. Sunlight was strong in summer and softened by thick cloud in the rainy season. Steady winds, mostly from the east and west, gave a sense of ventilation that eased both heat and humidity.

Warm without being extreme, rainy yet refreshing.
02

Data-driven analysis

I used Climate Consultant to plot temperature, wind velocity, sky cover and a psychrometric chart for Bangalore across all 12 months.

Temperature varies moderately through the year. The warmest period is March to May and the coolest is December and January, but the annual range stays narrow compared with many other locations, which keeps conditions relatively stable.

Wind velocity is generally low all year, with only slight increases during the monsoon months of June to September.

03

Comfort and cloud cover

On the psychrometric chart, a large share of the hourly data points cluster near the comfort zone, with few hours at the extremes of heat or cold. This shows how balanced the climate is across the seasons.

Cloud cover follows a clear seasonal cycle. Skies are clearer from December to March and much cloudier from June to September, during the southwest monsoon, before clearing again after it.

Overall, the data matched my experience: modest temperature swings, low wind velocity and a marked shift in cloud cover between the dry and monsoon seasons.

04

Design strategies for Bangalore

I translated each climate factor into design moves, using reference images to illustrate them.

  • Temperature and comfort: shade with overhangs, louvers or vegetation against warm March to May afternoons, and use cross-ventilation to take advantage of cooler evenings and nights.
  • Wind: orient openings to the predominant east and west breezes, and use courtyards and permeable layouts to channel airflow.
  • Radiation and sunlight: moderate to high solar radiation supports solar energy harvesting; light shelves and shaded glazing balance daylight and glare.
  • Cloud cover: plentiful diffuse light suits clerestories and skylights and reduces daytime artificial lighting.
  • Precipitation: heavy June to September rain calls for steep roofs, overhangs and good drainage, and makes rainwater harvesting effective.
  • Humidity: breathable facades, shaded outdoor spaces, and materials that do not trap moisture, such as stone and brick with lime plaster.
  • Ground and surface: pervious paving and landscaping to absorb monsoon rain, with native plants that cope with wet and dry periods.

What I took away

  • My memory of Bangalore's climate held up against the data: stable temperatures, low wind and a strong monsoon cycle.
  • Most hours sit near the comfort zone, so shading, ventilation and daylight do most of the work.
  • The monsoon drives the water strategies: steep roofs, drainage, rainwater harvesting and pervious ground.
