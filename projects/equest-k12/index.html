eQUEST Energy Modelling for a K-12 School

Three rounds of eQUEST simulation for a Connecticut school, from setting an ENERGY STAR target to testing envelope, geometry, lighting and equipment strategies under the ASHRAE 209 modelling cycle.

01

Setting an energy target

The Cognitive and Learning Center is a proposed 10,000 GSF, two-storey private school in Litchfield, Connecticut, with about 5,000 GSF per floor on a slab-on-grade. My first goal was to set a benchmark and find a design option that cuts energy use by at least 10% against the architect's original concept.

I entered the project as a K-12 school in ENERGY STAR Target Finder. It returned a design target site EUI of 53.8 kBtu/ft²·yr and source EUI of 91.1 kBtu/ft²·yr, with an estimated energy cost of $17,932.79 per year and 32.37 metric tons CO₂e per year. I used the 53.8 site EUI as the reference for every model that followed.

02

Baseline and early design strategies

I modelled the original concept in eQUEST with the DD Wizard: 40% window-to-wall ratio, DX single-zone rooftop units with gas furnaces, typical school ventilation rates, 0.7 W/ft² lighting and plug loads peaking near 0.5 W/ft². The baseline used 62,741 kWh of electricity and 705.63 MMBtu of gas per year, an approximate site EUI of 92.0 kBtu/ft²·yr. Space heating dominated gas use, as expected with gas furnaces.

I then tested four strategies against that baseline: reducing window area, adding roof insulation, a daylighting EEM, and changing the building shape. Window and roof changes gave moderate savings, daylighting had a clear effect on electricity, and changing the shape gave the largest cut in both fuels.

03

Choosing a preferred option

I selected Change Shape as the preferred option. It lowered electricity from 62,741 to 49,810 kWh and gas from 705.63 to 538.39 MMBtu, bringing site EUI to about 70.8 kBtu/ft²·yr. That is roughly a 23% reduction, more than twice the 10% goal.

It still sits above the 53.8 target, so further measures are needed in later phases. The main finding is that geometry, glazing, insulation and daylighting change performance a lot without touching the HVAC system type, which is why they belong early in design.

04

ASHRAE 209 load-reduction cycle

Next I worked through ASHRAE 209 Modeling Cycle #3, which tests load reductions before any HVAC change. I modelled a multi-wing, two-level school with perimeter and core zones in eQUEST 3.65, started in the Wizard and moved to Detailed Data Edit for parametric runs. I simulated it with Hartford, Connecticut TMY2 weather.

Each measure ran as its own parametric case off the same baseline, with HVAC held constant, so the effect of each one stays isolated. I tested lower solar gain glazing (DOE-2 glass type 2203), lighting power reduced from about 0.49 to 0.39 W/ft², and equipment power set to 0.40 W/ft² from about 0.50 W/ft².

05

What the load-reduction runs showed

Reducing lighting power was the strongest single measure: 3,246 kWh less electricity per year (about 4.4%), source EUI down from 97.69 to 94.96, and $552 per year in savings, with lower peak demand and cooling. Gas rose by 46 therms because less lighting heat was left to warm the spaces.

The low solar gain glazing cut peak cooling from 37 to 36 tons but raised gas use by 66 therms. In this heating-dominated climate, the next step is to optimise glazing for both U-factor and solar heat gain rather than simply darkening it.

In an earlier run of the same cycle, the equipment case moved the targeted end use the wrong way, from 72.5 to 78.5 MBtu. I treated that as a model-input diagnostic and flagged the space-level equipment inputs for review. In the later run the equipment case saved 1,110 kWh (about 1.5%), but I read it with care because one 0.40 W/ft² value was applied to spaces with different baseline densities, including a core space at 0.252 W/ft².

All three cases were modeled separately; their savings should not be added together without running a combined-measures model.

What I took away

  • Early geometry and envelope decisions moved site energy by about 23% without changing the HVAC system.
  • In a cold climate, cutting solar gain or internal heat lowers cooling but adds heating, so every measure needs a whole-building check.
  • Running measures as separate parametric cases from one baseline makes each result clear and exposes input errors.
  • Simulated preliminary results guide decisions; they are not measured savings.
