Mrittika is my B.Arch thesis: a pottery and sculptural experience centre meant to support the potter community while keeping their craft alive. I set out to give artisans workspaces suited to how they actually work, exhibition spaces that connect them to larger markets, and community spaces for teaching and collaboration. The design draws on vernacular principles, passive cooling, rainwater harvesting and local materials such as clay and bamboo.

01

Why a pottery centre

My thesis began with a simple chain of questions. Architecture led me to sustainability, and sustainability led me to ask how a building could support a craft community. The answer I chose was a pottery experience centre, with four objectives: cultural preservation, economic empowerment, artistic value and community development.

Potters face real socio-economic pressure. Many work from home studios that are cramped and poorly ventilated, with no proper storage, and they dry their pots on the roads. Their market is growing, but the number of potters is falling. Mrittika tries to answer that with one integrated place for making, showing, learning and living.

02

Understanding the community

I studied two living pottery communities. Pottery Town in Bengaluru was established during the reign of the Mysore king by gathering potters from Karnataka, Andhra, Kerala and Tamil Nadu. I looked at its population, the role of women in the craft, its street-side shops, open workshops, kilns and drying spaces, and the challenges of urbanisation, income and passing skills to younger generations.

Ramanagara, about 50 km from Bengaluru, is known for pottery because of its clay-rich soil. Here I focused on the artisans' techniques (hand-moulding, wheel throwing and kiln firing) and on how pottery enters architecture as terracotta tiles, jali work, facade ornament, bricks and blocks.

03

Learning from precedents

To understand the building type, I compared five precedents: Slow Pottery in Andhra Pradesh, Aura Pottery in Chandigarh, Dharamkot Studio in Himachal Pradesh, the Sanbao Ceramic Art Institute in China, and Rann Utsav in Gujarat. I read each one through the same parameters: location, landscape, relationship with nature, surrounding context, materials, zoning, site plan, volume, light and ventilation, and amenities.

  • A centre placed away from the city's bustle gives a sense of calm.
  • Clear zoning of workshops, exhibitions, residences and communal space makes the site easier to use.
  • Low-rise volumes that follow the topography keep the scale intimate.
  • Locally sourced materials tie the buildings to their place and culture.
04

Choosing the site

I compared three sites: Khanapur, Ramanagara and Kamalapura. I rated each on cultural significance, connectivity, market demand, skilled community and availability of raw materials. Ramanagara scored best overall. It has excellent connectivity to Bengaluru, a large nearby market and growing interest in eco-tourism and retreats.

On the site itself, the sun path runs east to west and the prevailing wind comes from the southwest. The residential area to the northeast is the main source of noise, so I planned vegetation along that edge. A nearby lake helps cool the site passively, and the surrounding trees give shade and a cool breeze.

05

Zoning around a pond

The zoning sets out the art gallery, the institute and the gathering spaces as the main zones, with a café, plaza and pavilion between them. Residential areas, parking and landscape complete the site. Everything is arranged around a calm central pond.

06

Shilpashraya, the art gallery

The gallery shows pottery and sculpture as cultural treasures and connects artists with visitors. Its plan is an S-shaped curve, which lets the exhibits unfold as a story. Display units have pockets between them to hold pottery and sculpture. Around the building I placed a sculptural garden, outdoor tactile exhibits that visitors can touch, a seating area and a pavilion.

07

Mrittika Vidyalaya, the art institute

The institute is the teaching hub for pottery, sculpture and traditional art forms. It sits between the gallery and the workshops and links the two. Its curved brick form follows the same fluid language as the rest of the site.

08

Karyalaya, the admin block

I based the admin block on a circle to make it central and easy to navigate. The concept moves from a community circle to a pottery wheel, then breaks the ring to form a courtyard. The plan wraps conference, record, admin and principal's rooms, offices, a pantry and storage around a central water court, with reception at the front.

09

Dhara Café

The café's curved layout comes from the fluid forms of pottery. I developed it from a linear courtyard through horizontal and square courtyards to a semi-circular one. Two curved wings face each other across a shared curved space. It uses eco-friendly materials and passive strategies, with indoor and outdoor seating, and serves as a cultural meeting point as well as a place to eat.

10

Mrittika Nivas, the hostel

The hostel houses students, guests and visiting artists in single and double rooms. It also has interactive lounges, a multipurpose hall and outdoor spaces, including an interaction space on the roof. Its long arched brick facade curves around the southern court and frames the plaza.

11

Master plan and plaza

The master plan brings the five blocks together with the entrance, parking, a gift shop, a plaza, pavilions, seating areas and an STP. A central plaza of canopy stalls gives potters a place to show and sell their work, with paths, planting and water running through it.

What I took away

  • Studying the community first gave the brief its purpose.
  • One formal language, curves drawn from the pottery wheel, held five very different blocks together.
  • Comparing sites with clear criteria made the choice of Ramanagara easy to defend.
