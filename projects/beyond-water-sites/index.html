Beyond Buildings and Water

A study of water at three scales: my personal footprint, my home's fixtures, and stormwater on Northeastern University's West Village campus.

Tools

I traced water use from my own footprint to my household and then to a campus site. For the West Village, I modelled the existing site in the National Stormwater Calculator and then tested a package of Low Impact Development (LID) controls. The changes cut average annual runoff by 11.02 inches and roughly doubled the share of wet days retained.

01

My personal water footprint

My total water footprint is 1,033 m³ a year, about 2,832 litres a day. That is somewhat lower than the average for many Americans. Diet and manufactured goods are likely the largest contributors, since they carry most indirect water use.

What surprised me was how much virtual water is embedded in everyday food and products. It made clear that personal sustainability has to count indirect water as well as what comes out of the tap.

02

Water use in my home

I live with two others in a building from 1956. I estimated our daily use fixture by fixture from flow rates, event counts and durations. The total came to 223.7 gallons a day, with the shower and kitchen sink using the most at 72 gallons each.

The total was higher than I expected, which showed me how quickly use adds up in a three-person home. The practical fixes are shorter showers, low-flow fixtures and repairing leaks promptly. I also compared my figure with an online estimate based on household size (87.41 × y^0.69 × 365), which gave about 66,216 gallons a year. That is much higher than my manual tally, which suggests either that our habits are more efficient than average or that the formula includes factors such as leaks.

The West Village site

The site is the West Village campus area bounded by Huntington Avenue, Parker Street, Ruggles Street, Leon Street and Hallenborg Way. I measured the roofs of Willis Hall, Khoury College, the West Village residence complexes, the Behrakis Health Sciences Center and the Admissions Visitor Center, then added roads, paths, parking and lawns.

Of the 8.11 acres I measured, 7.11 acres are impervious and only 1.0 acre is lawn or green space. I modelled the site in the National Stormwater Calculator as 9 acres on hydrologic soil group C (hydraulic conductivity 0.04 in/hr), 5% slope, 88% impervious and 12% lawn, using 20 years of rainfall data from Logan International Airport.

Baseline and altered stormwater scenarios

With no LID controls, the site sheds most of its rain. Average annual runoff was 33.89 inches out of 42.01 inches of rainfall, only 19.01% of wet days were retained, and the most rainfall the site could hold was 0.25 inches.

For the altered scenario I kept the same site and soil and added seven LID controls: disconnection (5%), rain harvesting (10%), rain gardens (5%), green roofs (40%), street planters (10%), infiltration basins (5%) and permeable pavement (10%). Runoff fell to 22.87 inches a year, retained wet days rose to 39.74%, and maximum rainfall retained increased to 0.72 inches.

05

Cost of the LID package

The baseline has no capital or maintenance cost because it has no LID controls. The altered scenario carries an estimated capital cost of about $2.15 million and about $80,000 a year in maintenance. Green roofs account for most of it, at $1.51 to $2.01 million in capital cost, followed by permeable pavement at $0.39 to $0.47 million.

I judged the investment worthwhile because it reduces stormwater discharge, improves groundwater recharge and makes the campus more resilient. Green roofs and permeable pavement do most of the work on infiltration and retention, while rain gardens and harvesting support water reuse.

What I took away

  • Indirect, virtual water made up a larger part of my footprint than I expected.
  • At 88% impervious, the West Village sends most of its rain straight to runoff.
  • A mix of seven LID controls cut runoff by 11.02 inches a year and doubled retention, with green roofs as both the largest lever and the largest cost.
