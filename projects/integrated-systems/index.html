Integrated Systems Section: Seattle School

A single building section for a south-facing classroom wing that brings daylight, passive solar heating, ventilation, acoustics, rainwater and solar power together in one design.

I designed a section through a south-facing classroom wing in Seattle to show how one building cut can carry many environmental systems at once. The butterfly roof, façade and slab work with the sun, rain and cool, cloudy climate to cut energy use while keeping students visually, thermally and acoustically comfortable.

01

Reading the Seattle climate

I started with the climate. Seattle is cool and mild all year, so heating demand is much larger than cooling demand. The sky is overcast on many days, which makes diffuse light the main daylight source, and rain falls steadily through the year.

The temperature range shows that the design does not need to fight extreme heat. The psychrometric chart shows that only about 6% of hours fall inside the comfort zone without help, and most of the rest are cool. That pointed me toward capturing winter sun with south glass and a heavy slab, and toward daylight and natural ventilation instead of large mechanical systems.

02

From climate drivers to design moves

I linked each climate factor to a specific response in the section, so every system in the drawing has a reason to be there.

03

The integrated section

The section cuts through two classrooms on either side of a central corridor. Deep south overhangs and U-0.20 glazing control solar gain and heat loss, and the operable windows are upgraded from U-0.38 to U-0.20. Low winter sun reaches an exposed 6-inch concrete slab that stores heat as thermal mass.

Daylight is organised in three zones across each classroom: a bright zone near the window that needs glare control, a main working zone with good daylight, and an inner zone that needs supplemental electric light. A central skylight over the corridor brings daylight into the middle of the plan.

Cool outdoor air enters through operable windows and warm air leaves through high-level vents and a mechanically operable skylight, using the stack effect. An acoustic ceiling with NRC about 0.8 and an acoustic wall control noise in the classrooms.

04

The roof as an energy and water system

The butterfly roof does three jobs. Its ridge pulls light and warm air to the centre through the skylight. Its upper surface carries a roof-mounted PV array that generates renewable electricity and lowers grid reliance. Its valley form sends rainwater outward to planted rain gardens at both ends, where the runoff infiltrates on site and supports the landscape.

  • Heating peak: 154.7 to 85.1 kBtu/hr
  • Cooling peak: 129.3 to 103.5 kBtu/hr
  • Annual EUI: 88.2 to 36.9 kBtu/ft²·yr (58% lower)
  • High-efficiency heat pump with COP about 4.0
The butterfly roof pulls light and air to the ridge, while PV on top and rain-to-garden drainage turn the roof into an energy and water system.

What I took away

  • Climate analysis first: Seattle's cool, cloudy, rainy conditions set the priorities for every system in the section.
  • One roof form can handle daylight, ventilation, solar power and stormwater together.
  • Passive moves (overhangs, thermal mass, daylight zoning, stack ventilation) carry most of the load before the heat pump and PV take over.
