LEED v5 EQc2: Occupant Experience

A research paper on LEED v5's new Occupant Experience credit: how it works, how it reweights indoor environmental quality, and why design documentation alone may not deliver comfort.

LEED v5 brings thermal comfort, daylight and views, acoustics, biophilic design and occupant control together in one credit, EQc2: Occupant Experience. In this paper I explain the credit's five options, show how it shifts points toward lighting and nature, and critique its reliance on design intent over measured outcomes. I close with five proposals to make the credit perform for real occupants.

01

From energy to quality of life

Attention to indoor conditions such as thermal comfort, air quality and daylight grew in the 1960s and 1970s, as building innovations and new office layouts changed workplaces (Hedge, 2000). Research since then has linked satisfaction with indoor environmental quality (IEQ) to health, wellbeing and job performance (Institute of Medicine, 2011; Bluyssen, 2014).

The U.S. Green Building Council began developing LEED in 1998 and included IEQ as a central category. LEED for New Construction evolved from version 2.0 in 2000 to version 4 in 2013 (Altomonte & Schiavon, 2018). Earlier versions prioritized energy efficiency and environmental performance. LEED v5, launched in April 2025, shifts toward what LEED calls "Quality of Life": occupant health, wellbeing, resilience and equity (Benholm, 2025).

v5 turns these goals into technical specifications such as glazing ratios, sightline distances and occupant feedback mechanisms. Whether those specifications reliably produce lasting occupant satisfaction is still an open, empirical question, and it is the question behind this paper.

To move beyond neutral or sufficient spaces toward human-centered design.
02

How the credit is structured

In LEED v3 and v4, thermal comfort, ventilation, daylight and acoustics were separate credits. v5 is the first version to merge them into one Occupant Experience framework and to make biophilic design and adaptable environments explicit, point-earning factors (USGBC, 2025; Benholm, 2025). Projects can combine pathways across all five options.

  • Biophilic Environment (1–4 points): one point for integrated biophilic design that shows five core principles, or 2–3 points for quality views from 75% or 90% of regularly occupied floor area. Views must pass through glass with visible light transmittance above 40% and include nature, landmarks, art or objects at least 25 feet away.
  • Adaptable Environment (1–2 points): occupant choice in thermal, sound and lighting conditions plus at least one accessible quiet space, with one or three additional strategies such as height-adjustable desks, varied furniture or outdoor space within 200 feet of entrances.
  • Thermal Environment (1 point): meet ASHRAE 55-2023 and document solutions for seasonal change, avoiding overcooling, occupants arriving from other conditions, personal customization and different activity levels.
  • Sound Environment (1–2 points): map acoustic expectations for each space across noise exposure, acoustic comfort, privacy, communication and soundscape, or show by calculation, modeling or measurement that criteria are met in at least 75% of occupied spaces and all core learning spaces.
  • Lighting Environment (1–6 points): glare control with manual override; quality electric lighting (UGR of 19 or lower, CRI of at least 90 or IES TM-30-20 targets); at least 30% of occupied area within 20 feet of glazing; and daylight simulation to IES LM-83-23, scored on average sDA.

03

A new hierarchy of comfort

In v4, most projects could earn 6–7 IEQ points related to comfort and experience, spread across credits such as controllability of systems, thermal comfort, daylight and views, and acoustic performance, each usually worth one point. No single category dominated.

v5 redistributes these points. Lighting and biophilic environment together can account for up to 10 points, while thermal and sound together offer only 2–3. This signals a deliberate re-prioritization of visual experience and connection to nature over thermal and acoustic comfort (USGBC, 2013; USGBC, 2025).

Compliance is shown through narratives, templates, drawings, simulation reports, specifications and photographs. Because v5 also stresses continuous monitoring and performance data, facility managers need to be involved early. Industry estimates put certification under v5 at about 10 months.

04

Where the credit falls short

Post-occupancy evaluation research supports this critique. Privacy, control and access to quiet space are more strongly linked to satisfaction than overall comfort averages (Parkinson et al., 2023). POE creates a feedback loop between buildings and their users (Zimmerman & Martin, 2001), and the questionnaire survey is the most widely used method because it is simple and cost-effective (Heinzerling et al., 2013).

  • Performance vs. intent gap: compliance rests on design intent, not measured outcomes. A study of more than 11,000 occupants in 93 LEED buildings found that earning specific IEQ credits did not reliably raise satisfaction with those aspects (Altomonte et al., 2019). LEED v2 and v3 had an optional thermal comfort survey credit, later removed; v5 could have strengthened it with standardized tools and response-rate thresholds.
  • Limited personalization: adaptable environments and occupant controls are optional, so many occupants still lack meaningful control, and dissatisfaction persists in certified buildings. Personal controls such as operable windows or local thermostats significantly improve reported satisfaction (Kim, Schiavon & Brager, 2018).
  • Overly generic framework: thresholds are largely the same across climates, cultures and building types, although priorities differ, for example glare in GCC climates or acoustic privacy in open offices.
  • Documentation over performance: the system rewards documentation and simulation but has no mandatory post-occupancy evaluation, such as user surveys or IAQ measurements.
  • Equity and inclusion gaps: focusing on "regularly occupied" spaces can miss the needs of marginalized users, people with disabilities and non-standard work patterns.
05

Proposals

LEED v5 raises the bar on what "green" means, with continuous IAQ tracking, occupant feedback and more responsibility for the people who run buildings day to day. It also means longer timelines and new challenges for facilities with legacy systems or tight budgets. Its focus on occupant experience will only matter if it changes everyday practice, not just documentation.

  • Mandatory post-occupancy verification: occupant surveys and IEQ measurements 12–18 months after handover.
  • Enhanced personal control over thermal, lighting and acoustic conditions, including operable windows, individual thermostats, adjustable lighting and quiet refuge areas.
  • Contextual flexibility: pathways tailored to building type, climate and culture, such as glare and solar gain strategies for hot, arid regions.
  • Stronger inclusion criteria covering accessibility, neurodiversity and non-standard user groups through universal design and user engagement.
  • Outcome-based scoring: award some points for measured results such as comfort survey scores and air quality monitoring.

What I took away

  • LEED v5 weights lighting and nature far above thermal and acoustic comfort.
  • Earning IEQ credits does not guarantee satisfied occupants; measured outcomes should count.
  • Personal control and post-occupancy feedback are the clearest routes to closing the performance gap.
