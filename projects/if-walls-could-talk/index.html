If These Walls Could Talk

A thermal and moisture study of a brick cavity wall at Richards Hall, Northeastern University, using thermography, hand calculations and Building Science Advisor to compare two retrofits.

01

Reading the wall with a thermal camera

Richards Hall is a masonry building on the Northeastern University campus. Prof. Fannon captured thermal images of its façade with a FLIR camera on 8 October. The infrared views show surface temperature across the brick and glazing and gave me a starting point for looking at where heat moves through the envelope.

02

The wall assembly

I drew the typical wall section from outside to inside. Each layer has a job: the brick sheds rain and wind, the vented air gap drains and dries the cavity, the insulation slows heat flow, the concrete block carries the structure, and the gypsum board gives a finished, fire-resistant interior surface.

  • Exterior brick, 100 mm (4 in)
  • Vented air gap, 50 mm (2 in)
  • Cavity insulation, mineral wool or fiberglass, 50 mm (2 in)
  • Concrete / concrete block (CMU), 200 mm (8 in)
  • Gypsum board interior finish, 15.9 mm (5/8 in)
03

R-value, U-factor and code check

I added the R-value of each layer plus the standard interior and exterior air films. The total came to R-13.90, which gives a U-factor of about 0.072 Btu/hr·ft²·°F. The Massachusetts code (780 CMR Table 502.1.2) allows a maximum of U-0.080 for above-grade mass walls, so the wall passes.

This result depends on the assumed thicknesses. Thinner insulation or an unvented air space would lower the R-value, so the numbers should be checked against measured dimensions.

04

Dewpoint and condensation risk

To find where condensation could form, I used a 70 °F interior and the 9 °F Boston winter design temperature. Interior air at 70 °F and 50% relative humidity has a dewpoint of about 50 °F. I split the 61 °F total difference across each layer in proportion to its share of the total R-value.

The temperature stays above 50 °F through the gypsum and the concrete block and drops below it in the insulation, on the cold side of the CMU. With a vapor retarder on the warm side, condensation risk inside the structure is low. Any moisture would condense in the brick and air gap zone, where it can drain and dry to the outside.

05

Testing retrofits in Building Science Advisor

I then modelled the existing wall and two retrofits in the DOE Building Science Advisor for Boston (Zone 5A, cool-humid). BSA rated the existing wall at R-21 with low mold risk, but its moisture score of 21 is above the limit of 16, which puts it in the risky range for long-term durability.

For the exterior retrofit I added 1 inch of exterior mineral fiber insulation (3 inches in total) and an impermeable exterior finish. For the interior retrofit I added interior mineral wool insulation with a suitable vapor retarder. Both passed all three criteria.

06

Recommendation

I recommend the exterior retrofit for this masonry wall. It moves the dewpoint outward, allows continuous insulation and better air and water barrier detailing, and gives the strongest moisture result. It may be limited by preservation rules, site constraints or cost.

The interior retrofit is a workable option where the façade cannot change. It can be phased with interior renovation, but it needs careful vapor retarder detailing and air sealing, and it takes some floor area.

What I took away

  • A wall can meet the energy code for U-value and still carry a moisture durability risk.
  • A layer-by-layer thermal gradient shows exactly where the dewpoint falls in an assembly.
  • Combining hand calculations with simulation made it faster to compare retrofit options on energy, mold and moisture together.
  • Vapor retarder placement and material compatibility decide whether a retrofit succeeds.
