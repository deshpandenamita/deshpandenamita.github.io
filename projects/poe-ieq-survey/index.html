Post Occupancy Evaluation: IEQ Self Survey

A seven-day personal thermal comfort study that compares how warm I felt with what a datalogger measured, and tests the result against ASHRAE 55.

Method
Ecological Momentary Assessment (self-survey)
Tools
Portable temperature datalogger, self-survey log, scatter plots
Location
Home, gym, Snell Library, stores, restaurants and a friend's house

November 24–30, 2025

Standards such as ASHRAE 55 and LEED set comfort ranges for buildings, but real occupants often feel differently. For seven days I surveyed my own thermal comfort five times a day while carrying a temperature logger. The data showed that I am most satisfied and most productive when I feel warm, at 22–24 °C.

01

Why study one person

Thermal comfort affects occupant satisfaction, health and productivity. Design standards give prescriptive ranges, yet there is a known gap between predicted and actual experience. I wanted to see that gap at the level of one person in real settings.

I used an Ecological Momentary Assessment approach. Instead of surveying many people in one building, I tracked one person, myself, across the different indoor spaces I used in a normal week. I combined two data streams: subjective survey responses and objective temperature logging.

02

Method

From November 24 to 30, 2025, covering weekdays and a weekend, I filled in a survey at 9 AM, 12 PM, 3 PM, 6 PM and 9 PM. That gave 35 entries. I only answered indoors, in non-transient spaces where I had been for at least 10 minutes: home, the gym, Snell Library, stores on Newbury Street, Quincy Market and Assembly, restaurants in Woburn and downtown, and a friend's house.

A portable datalogger recorded air temperature every 5 minutes for the whole week, more than 1,150 readings. Indoor temperatures ranged from 18.44 °C to 29.51 °C; including outdoor time, the full range was 15.3 °C to 30.7 °C. I matched each survey to the nearest logged reading and plotted three relationships, then compared my preferred range with the ASHRAE 55 comfort zone.

  • Thermal sensation: ASHRAE 55 seven-point scale, –3 (cold) to +3 (hot)
  • Thermal satisfaction: 1 (very satisfied) to 7 (very dissatisfied)
  • Clothing: 0.5 clo for gym wear or very light clothing, 0.8 clo for a typical indoor outfit
  • Perceived productivity: 1 (low) to 3 (high)
03

Satisfaction peaks when I feel warm

Most of my sensation votes fell between +1 and +3, so the spaces I visited generally felt warm to me. My best satisfaction ratings (1–2) clustered at a sensation of +2, "warm". Satisfaction dropped to 5–7 when I felt hot (+3), especially while cooking at a friend's house on November 27 and on a warm morning on November 28.

04

My internal thermometer

Comparing sensation with measured temperature, I reported +2 ("warm") consistently between about 22 °C and 24 °C. Above 25–26 °C I reliably felt hot (+3). Below 21 °C I reported only +1 or 0, which suggests I am more sensitive to cooler rooms than the ASHRAE baseline assumes.

05

Comfort and productivity

Productivity was highest (level 3) in the 22–24 °C range, the same band as my highest satisfaction. It fell to levels 1–2 at the extremes, above 26 °C and below 20 °C. Cooler evenings around 18–19 °C noticeably lowered my productivity, and lighter clothing (0.5 clo) during the 29.5 °C cooking event did not restore comfort.

Compared with ASHRAE 55, my comfort zone sits slightly higher. Standard models might predict dissatisfaction at +2, but that is where I was most satisfied. For me, a typical winter setpoint of 21 °C would likely mean lower productivity, while 23 °C would likely give the best comfort and output.

Feeling distinctly warm was associated with greater comfort than feeling neutral.

What I took away

  • A neutral sensation (0) is not always the most comfortable one; for me, +2 was.
  • My optimal band was narrow, 22–24 °C, and comfort fell quickly outside 20–26 °C.
  • Clothing adjustment alone could not fix an overheated room.
  • Adaptive, personal controls can close the gap between generalized standards and individual comfort.
