# MoneyMap video scripts (draft for review)

Each video is about 75–100 seconds at a calm narrator pace (~140 words per minute).
**Narration** is what the voice says (also shown as captions). **Screen** is what you see.
The visuals use the palette you pick, and every video ends with: "This is for learning, not financial advice."

---

## Video 1 — Compound interest: money that grows on its own (~90 sec)

| # | Screen | Narration |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Title card. A single coin drops onto the screen. | Meet one thousand dollars. Let's see what happens if we just leave it alone and let it grow. |
| 2 | Coin goes into a jar labeled "8% a year". | Say it earns eight percent a year. That's a bit below what the US stock market has averaged over long periods in the past. Real years go up and down, though. |
| 3 | Year 1 bar appears: $1,000 blue + $80 orange. | After one year, you earn eighty dollars. Now you have one thousand and eighty. |
| 4 | Year 2 bar, with a small arrow from the orange part. | Here's the magic. In year two, you earn eight percent on the whole one thousand and eighty, including last year's growth. That's interest on interest. It's called compounding. |
| 5 | Bars race forward to year 10, with a counter showing $2,159. | After ten years, you have about two thousand one hundred sixty dollars. Your money has doubled. |
| 6 | Bars continue to year 30, with the counter ticking up to $10,063. | Keep going to thirty years, and you have about ten thousand dollars. Ten times your money, without adding a single cent. |
| 7 | A dashed "simple interest" line appears at $3,400. | Without compounding, earning eight percent on just the original thousand, you'd have only three thousand four hundred. |
| 8 | Two people on a timeline: one starts at 25, one at 35. | The secret ingredient is time. Starting ten years earlier can make a bigger difference than investing more later. |
| 9 | A red credit card with a growing pile. | But watch out. Compounding works against you with debt, too. Credit card interest grows the same way. |
| 10 | End card: "Start early. Be patient." | So start early, be patient, and let time do the heavy lifting. This is for learning, not financial advice. |

## Video 2 — Why do stock prices move? (~85 sec)

| # | Screen | Narration |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Title card. A price tag reading $50 wobbles. | Why does a stock's price go up and down every day? It's simpler than it looks. |
| 2 | Buyers in blue on the left, sellers in orange on the right, balanced. | A stock price is just the last price where a buyer and a seller agreed to trade. Right now, buyers and sellers are balanced, at fifty dollars. |
| 3 | A newspaper flies in: "Sales beat expectations!" Buyers multiply. | Now, good news. The company sold more than expected. Suddenly, more people want to buy. |
| 4 | Buyers raise their hands; the tag climbs $52, $54, $58. A line chart draws upward. | With so many buyers competing, they offer higher prices. Sellers notice, and ask for more. The price rises until enough sellers say yes. |
| 5 | Newspaper: "Product recall." Sellers multiply and the tag drops. | Then, bad news. A product problem. Now lots of owners want to sell, and few want to buy. Sellers must accept lower prices, and the price falls. |
| 6 | The tag settles at $46 and the two sides rebalance. | At some point, buyers think it's a bargain again, and things balance out. |
| 7 | A tug-of-war rope between the two groups. | Nobody sets the price. It's a tug-of-war between buyers and sellers that never stops. News, fear, and excitement all pull on the rope. |
| 8 | End card: "Short term: mood. Long term: business." | In the short run, prices follow moods. In the long run, they tend to follow how well the business actually does. This is for learning, not financial advice. |

## Video 3 — Diversification: don't put all your eggs in one basket (~80 sec)

| # | Screen | Narration |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Title card. One basket holding all the eggs. | You've probably heard, "Don't put all your eggs in one basket." Here's what that means for investing. |
| 2 | A grid of 20 company tiles, all gray. | Here are twenty companies. Nobody knows which ones will do well this year. Not even the experts. |
| 3 | The tiles flip to green and red results: +35% ... -40%. | A year later, results are all over the place. One went up thirty-five percent. Another fell forty percent. |
| 4 | Two paths: one lands on $1,350, the other on $600. | If you put all one thousand dollars into one company, your result is pure luck. You could end with thirteen hundred fifty dollars, or just six hundred. |
| 5 | $1,000 splits into twenty $50 coins that spread across the tiles. | Now spread it out. Fifty dollars into each of the twenty. |
| 6 | The gains and losses cancel into one number: +8% = $1,080. | The big wins and the big losses mostly cancel out. You get the average, about eight percent. One thousand and eighty dollars. |
| 7 | A whole-market dip, shown as all the tiles dimming slightly. | Diversifying doesn't stop the whole market from falling sometimes. But it protects you from one bad company sinking everything. |
| 8 | End card: "Spread it out." | Less risk, similar average reward. That's diversification. This is for learning, not financial advice. |

## Video 4 — How an index fund works (~90 sec)

| # | Screen | Narration |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Title card. Hundreds of tiny company dots. | There are thousands of companies you could invest in. Picking winners is hard. What if you didn't have to pick? |
| 2 | Some dots light up and a list appears: "S&P 500". | An index is just a list. The S&P 500 is a list of about five hundred large US companies. |
| 3 | The lit dots fly into a basket labeled "Index fund". | An index fund buys every company on that list automatically. No guessing. |
| 4 | Inside the basket, a few dots grow bigger. | Bigger companies get a bigger slice. This is called being weighted by market cap, which just means company size. |
| 5 | A person buys one ticket, "1 share of the fund", with lines to every dot. | When you buy one share of the fund, you own a tiny piece of all five hundred companies at once. |
| 6 | Two fee bars: $5 versus $75. | Because a computer just follows the list, costs are low. On ten thousand dollars, a 0.05 percent fee is about five dollars a year. A typical actively managed fund might charge seventy-five dollars or more. |
| 7 | Real weekly SPY prices, 2019 to today, with the 2020 and 2022 dips marked. | The index has had scary drops, like in 2020 and 2022. But over long periods, it has historically grown. There are no guarantees, though. |
| 8 | End card: "Own a little of everything." | Own a little of everything, keep costs low, and stay patient. That's why so many people start with index funds. This is for learning, not financial advice. |
